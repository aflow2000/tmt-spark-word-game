AI Data Centers Are Running Into the Power Wall

Racks that drew 5–10 kW a few years ago are being specified at 100 kW and beyond, and campuses once measured in megawatts are now planned in gigawatts. The constraint on AI growth is shifting from chips and capital to electricity and cooling.

Why it matters

Site selection is becoming an energy question first and a real estate question second. Power availability — not land price — now sets where AI capacity gets built, how fast it delivers and what it costs.

Data CentersInfrastructure 3 min read

The 800V Data Center

To feed ever-denser racks, operators are rethinking the electrical backbone of the building — moving from traditional lower-voltage AC distribution toward higher-voltage DC. Fewer conversions mean less wasted energy, less copper and less heat.

Why it matters

A different electrical architecture changes switchgear, busways, commissioning and the skills contractors need on site. New builds and retrofits will diverge — and the supply chain will follow the new spec.

Industry impacted
Data centersElectrical contractorsEquipment makers
EnergyCapital Markets 4 min read

Nuclear Meets AI

Hyperscalers are signing long-term power agreements with existing nuclear plants and backing small modular reactors. AI demand wants firm, carbon-free power at gigawatt scale — and interconnection queues are too slow for AI timelines.

Why it matters

Energy strategy is becoming a core part of digital infrastructure planning. Decade-long power bets will shape where campuses are sited, who partners with whom, and which markets win the next wave of investment.

Industry impacted
EnergyData centersInvestorsRegulators
SemiconductorsConstruction 5 min read

The Semiconductor Construction Boom

Multibillion-dollar fab projects are reshaping construction demand across the United States. A leading-edge fab is among the most complex buildings ever delivered — cleanrooms, vibration control, ultra-pure water and thousands of skilled trades on one site.

Why it matters

Fab mega-projects absorb skilled labor, equipment and materials for years, tightening cost and schedule for everyone building near them. Suppliers and housing follow the fabs — and so does regional real estate demand.

Industry impacted
SemiconductorsConstructionRegional markets
AIRobotics 3 min read

Robotics Leave the Lab

Foundation models are reaching into the physical world. Humanoid and mobile robots are moving from demo videos to paid pilots in manufacturing and logistics — the first commercial test of "physical AI" at scale.

Why it matters

If robots work, industrial space changes: more power, different layouts, new safety zones and different labor profiles. Early adopters will quietly re-spec warehouses and plants over the next lease cycle.

The TMT RFP Playbook

How we open, price, schedule and question in TMT pursuits — grounded in what the industry told Spark this quarter.

14
Current version
All sectorsEvery service Updated v14 Reviewed Sep 2026

Open with their constraint, not our credentials.

The finding

Across this quarter's conversations, executives opened with the same three constraints — power, long-lead equipment and schedule risk — and none opened with a vendor's history. Interconnection queues beyond four years and rack densities past 130 kW are the numbers on their minds.

SourcesInterview · AI infrastructure lead
Best current practice

The executive summary leads with the client's constraint and what it means for their dates — then teaches one thing they didn't know, with a dated citation. Credentials move to the appendix; understanding is the credential.

Draft language

"Your program's critical path now runs through the grid, not the ground. With interconnection queues in primary markets stretching past four years and rack densities moving beyond 130 kW, we have built this response around the two questions that will decide your schedule: when your power arrives, and what your equipment strategy assumes."

Data CentersProcurement Current Reviewed Sep 2026

Procurement-first scheduling is the default.

The finding

Transformer and switchgear lead times are still running 24+ months, and panelists were unanimous: equipment now drives the construction schedule, not the other way around. Owners who released long-lead packages before design completion held their dates; those who didn't, slipped.

SourcesProcurement roundtable transcripts
Best current practice

Every data center response presents a procurement-first baseline: long-lead electrical equipment as the program's first decision, an owner-furnished equipment strategy we manage, and early-release packages shown explicitly in the schedule.

Draft language

"We recommend treating long-lead electrical equipment as the first procurement decision of the program. With transformer and switchgear lead times still running beyond 24 months, our baseline schedule releases those packages ahead of design completion — under an owner-furnished strategy we will structure and manage on your behalf."

Data CentersCost Management Updated v14 Reviewed Sep 2026

Price the next-generation spec, not last cycle's.

The finding

Liquid-ready is now the default specification, and higher-voltage DC distribution is moving from concept to reference design. Cost plans built on air-cooled, lower-voltage benchmarks are quietly obsolete — and retrofit economics are diverging sharply from new-build.

SourcesVendor briefing transcripts
Best current practice

Cost proposals carry a split baseline — legacy versus AI-ready specification — with an explicit electrical labor premium reflecting current market capacity, and a stated position on where retrofit stops making sense.

Draft language

"Our cost plan carries the industry's specification shift explicitly: a split baseline for legacy and liquid-ready designs, an electrical labor premium reflecting today's market capacity, and a clear view of where retrofit economics diverge from new-build — so the number you approve is the number the market will actually deliver."

SemiconductorsProgram Management Current Reviewed Aug 2026

Model the labor draw before the client asks.

The finding

Fab mega-projects absorb skilled trades for years and reshape every construction market they touch. A fab program lead put it plainly on the record: "labor is the schedule." Escalation near fab hubs is outrunning national indices.

Sources
Best current practice

Responses for fab and fab-adjacent work include a regional labor draw analysis — availability, escalation and mobilization phasing measured against the mega-projects competing for the same crews.

Draft language

"Fab-scale projects reshape every trade market they touch. Our response includes a regional labor draw analysis — availability, escalation and mobilization phasing against the programs competing for the same crews — so your schedule reflects the market you will actually build in, not a national average."

AI OccupiersProject Management Updated v14 Reviewed Sep 2026

Sell speed-to-occupancy; phase everything.

The finding

AI occupiers doubling headcount told us the expensive risk is a late floor, not a change order — and that requirements will keep moving after award. Speed of fit-out has become a competitive weapon in the talent war.

SourcesTranscript · occupier interviews
Best current practice

Occupier proposals are structured around occupancy dates: phased delivery, early-release long-lead packages, and a decision calendar that tells the client exactly which choices protect which dates.

Draft language

"For a team doubling headcount, the expensive risk is a late floor, not a change order. We propose phased delivery with early-release long-lead packages and a decision calendar that protects your occupancy dates while your requirements keep moving — because they will."

Energy & PowerProgram Advisory Current Reviewed Sep 2026

Speak the client's energy language.

The finding

Energy strategy has become inseparable from program strategy: PPAs, behind-the-meter generation and SMR options are entering scope conversations that used to end at the utility handoff. Clients notice which advisors can hold that conversation.

Sources
Best current practice

Governance sections carry the client's energy milestones inside the master schedule and risk register — interconnection dates, PPA dependencies, behind-the-meter decisions — rather than treating power as someone else's scope.

Draft language

"We have structured governance to sit alongside your energy strategy: interconnection milestones, power agreement dependencies and behind-the-meter decisions are carried in the master schedule and risk register — not treated as someone else's scope that arrives as a surprise."

All sectorsClarifications Updated v14 Reviewed Sep 2026

Ask what the roundtables taught us to ask.

The finding

The sharpest questions in this quarter's sessions came from the industry itself. They now form our standard clarification set — asked in every pursuit, because each one changes the shape of the right response.

SourcesInterview archive · Q3
Best current practice — the standard clarification set
  • Q1What is your confirmed power position — interconnection status, energization dates, and who owns that risk today?
  • Q2Which long-lead equipment is already secured, and what assumptions sit behind those delivery dates?
  • Q3What rack density and cooling basis of design should we plan against — and how firm is it?
  • Q4Where does this program sit in your capital plan if AI demand shifts — what accelerates, what pauses?
  • Q5Who else is building near you, and what will that do to labor, logistics and escalation?

The Spark voice

Educate-first — even in a proposal.

The philosophy that runs the platform runs the playbook: a response should teach the reader something true about their own program.

In every response — avoid

1

Opening with firm history and organization charts.

2

Generic risk registers that could describe any project anywhere.

3

Undated market claims — "costs are rising," "lead times are long."

4

Pitching services before demonstrating understanding.

Reads like everyone else's response.

In every response — do

1

Open with the client's constraint — power, labor, schedule or capital — and what it means for their dates.

2

Teach one thing they don't already know, and date it.

3

Cite live market intelligence with sources — "Spark, Sept 2026," not "recent trends."

4

Name the next conversation, not just the contract.

Reads like the team that already understands the program.

The TMT Spark network effect

Two ways to meet an executive.

The platform doesn't replace business development — it replaces the cold open. Compare the paths.

Traditional outreach

1

Cold email. "Can Turner & Townsend help you manage your next construction project?"

2

Sales meeting request. A calendar invite from someone they've never met.

3

Low response rate. The message competes with every other vendor in the inbox.

Transactional from the first word — and easy to ignore.

The TMT Spark approach

1

Industry research. Spark's editorial work surfaces who is building, expanding and investing.

2

Invite them onto TMT Spark. A panel, a roundtable, an interview — a reason to talk that isn't a pitch.

3

Discuss their expertise. The conversation is about their world: their constraints, their plans, their view of the market.

4

Build the relationship. Shared interests, real content, follow-ups that feel natural.

5

Understand their priorities. Growth plans, cost pressures, infrastructure constraints — heard firsthand.

6

Introduce Turner & Townsend capabilities when appropriate. Only where Turner & Townsend can actually help — and only when the timing is right.

Reciprocal from the first word — and worth responding to.

"We're interviewing leaders about how AI companies are managing the infrastructure constraints created by rapid growth. We'd love your perspective."
